The Clerical Chokepoint

Vessel data reveal how Tehran is rerouting the world's tankers into a private gauntlet. By turning a global commons into a toll road, Iran is forcing a reluctant White House to choose between a humiliating retreat or a hazardous escalation.

April 19, 2026 | Live AIS data, OFAC SDN cross-reference, OilPrice API, NASA FIRMS VIIRS

Before February 28, the Strait of Hormuz was the world’s busiest oil chokepoint. Roughly 134 vessels passed through it each day. Crude tankers carrying about a fifth of the world’s petroleum, liquefied natural gas carriers feeding utilities from Tokyo to Madrid, and cargo ships calling at every Gulf port.

Brent crude sat at $71.82 a barrel. War-risk insurance was a routine line on every charter agreement. The Lloyd’s of London designation marking the Gulf as a war zone was a paperwork formality, not a barrier to commerce.

February 28, 2026

Then the war started. Traffic collapsed overnight.

30

Transits on March 1, down 78% in 24 hours

Within the first week of March the strait was effectively closed. On March 21 the platform recorded no transits at all. A full shutdown of a waterway through which a fifth of the world’s oil normally moves. Brent climbed to $117 a barrel, a 63-percent war premium that priced in not just the lost barrels but the prospect of a wider regional war.

The pattern is stark. February shows a busy strait, a daily flow of roughly 134 vessels, split evenly between inbound tankers and outbound ships carrying oil and LNG to global markets. On March 1 the bars collapse. There are stretches in March when no vessels cross at all. The yellow line, Brent crude, climbs in lockstep, reflecting the global price of oil when its single most important chokepoint stops functioning.

EchoFrame’s pipeline reconstructs this curve nightly from raw AIS vessel tracks (Datalastic), matched against a fixed longitude line at 56.50°E, the same virtual-gate methodology the Joint Maritime Information Center publishes. Vessels are only counted when their tracks actually cross the line at sustained speed; idling at anchor doesn’t qualify. The collapse is therefore not a question of vessels being temporarily slowed by congestion, it is the absence of completed transits, a measurable structural break.

By mid-April some traffic has resumed, but at a fraction of normal volume, but increasingly along the standard southern lane rather than the Iranian corridor. Cross-referenced against Sentinel-1 SAR radar passes, at least 26 tankers have left the Gulf since February 28 with their AIS transponders disabled, vessels invisible to normal tracking, detectable only as radar returns. The visible fleet on the chart is therefore the lower bound of what is actually moving.

The safe corridor

The vessels that still transit are being funnelled through a single northern lane, and that lane runs inside Iranian territorial waters.

Instead of following the standard IMO Traffic Separation Scheme, the southern lane between Musandam and Larak Island that has governed traffic through the chokepoint for forty years, vessels are being diverted along a corridor inside Iranian territorial waters.

The geography is the story. The international shipping lane runs along the Omani side of the strait, the Traffic Separation Scheme that has governed the chokepoint for forty years. By mid-April most of the recovering traffic is using it: in the 48-hour window above, 23 transits, more than two-thirds of the total, crossed on the southern lane. The remaining 10 transits hugged the Iranian coast in the northern corridor, and the flag of those ten matters. Vessels in Iranian territorial waters are subject to Iranian inspection, Iranian boarding, and Iranian discretion. Who routes north and who routes south, and what each route implies about flag, cargo, and operator, is the maritime story this platform is built to track.

The map above tells two stories at once. The first is recovery: vessel transits have climbed back to roughly 11 a day, around 8 percent of the pre-conflict baseline, after weeks at zero. The second is sorting: those vessels are not moving as one fleet. Two thirds are passing on the internationally recognised southern lane, an effective return to normal routing for unflagged and allied tonnage. The remaining third is operating under Iranian air and missile cover in the northern corridor, and the operators choosing that route are not random.

Case in point

One ship in the snapshot above is highlighted in gold.

ARTMAN, IMO 9405930, is an OFAC-designated Iranian-flagged Supramax bulker that cleared EchoFrame’s automated sanctions-watch filter on the evening of April 18. The filter is mechanical: the OFAC Specially Designated Nationals list is cross-referenced against live AIS feeds, and every vessel that satisfies both filters at the moment it crosses the strait is escalated for analyst review. ARTMAN departed Bandar Abbas Anchorage at 15:56 UTC loaded to a 12.8-metre draught, transited the strait via the Iran-administered corridor at 10 knots, and as of this writing is underway in the Gulf of Oman bound for Kandla, India. She has not been interdicted.

The case tests the US blockade. Under the CENTCOM framing communicated April 13, the blockade applies to vessels entering or leaving Iranian ports and to vessels that have paid Iranian transit tolls; freedom of navigation is preserved for everything else. ARTMAN satisfies every qualifying criterion. Three reads are plausible. The first is that interdiction is simply pending: she is only now entering the Gulf of Oman intercept zone, and CENTCOM’s running tally of turned-around vessels stood at 23 as of midday April 18. The second is a tacit India carve-out, with Washington avoiding friction with New Delhi over Hormuz. The third is a spoofed destination, with a ship-to-ship transfer planned outside Iranian waters; IRISL bulkers have a documented history of declaring Indian or Pakistani ports and diverting. Arrival confirmation at Kandla on April 22, or its absence, will distinguish them.

The blockade’s credibility, and the leverage Tehran can claim over the chokepoint, both hinge on what happens next.

The damage is not contained to shipping. On March 18, Iranian missile and drone strikes hit Trains 4 and 6 at the Ras Laffan LNG complex in Qatar. The strike took 12.8 million tonnes of annual liquefaction capacity offline, roughly 13 percent of Gulf supply, and a meaningful share of the gas that Japanese, South Korean, and European utilities buy on long-term contracts. The operator declared force majeure. Charterers are rerouting LNG carriers around the Cape of Good Hope, adding seven to ten days per voyage. On the oil side, three regional bypass pipelines, running through Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Iran itself, can shunt 6.8 million barrels a day around the strait entirely. But they were built for emergencies, and they run near their design ceiling when fully used.

The damage at Ras Laffan was verified independently, without commercial satellite imagery, which was restricted across the complex in the days after the strike. EchoFrame triangulated three open data streams: NASA FIRMS detected thermal anomalies at the train locations on the night of the strike; Sentinel-1 SAR radar passes showed change-detection signatures consistent with structural damage at the same coordinates; and AIS tracking of the three Q-Max LNG carriers dedicated to the facility showed schedule deviations and prolonged idle periods inconsistent with normal loading operations. ACLED and ISW event records placed the incident in the same 24-hour window. No single source would have been sufficient; together they converge.

Now

The ceasefire holds in form only. Traffic has partially recovered, but the strait is under Iranian operational control, not international law.

Brent is trading $19 above its pre-conflict baseline. Lloyd’s war-risk premiums for hull insurance, which moved from a quarter of a percent of vessel value to over one percent in the first 48 hours of the war, have not retraced. Gas buyers in Northeast Asia are paying spot prices that reflect a structural absence of Qatari cargoes through 2026. The shooting has stopped; the redistribution of risk and cost it produced has not.

Whether the new arrangement holds, or breaks into a second war, depends on whether Tehran judges that the leverage it now exercises over the chokepoint is durable enough to convert into political concessions before Washington treats reopening it as a casus belli. That is a question this platform will keep grading against its own forecasts, six hours at a time, in public.
